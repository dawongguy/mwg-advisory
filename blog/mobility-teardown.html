Product review  ·  pliability · GOWOD · Bend  ·  Diagnosis, progression & pricing power

Nobody Will Let Me Set the Right Goal

Two of these three measure my body and score it. Neither will let me aim at one. Six weeks later I found the missing half built and hidden — which turns out to be the whole story.

Mike Wong · MWG Advisory Product Teardown October 2026
01Premise

I installed three mobility apps on the same day and ran them side by side for six weeks: pliability, GOWOD and Bend. I wanted to know whether any had built a coaching loop, or whether they were content libraries with a stretch timer attached.

A mobility subscription runs $20 a month at the top of this market, and the substitute isn't a cheaper app — it's free. The same stretches sit on YouTube at a price of zero. So a paid app has to sell something a video can't, and there's only one candidate: the service around the content. Somebody who looks at my body, decides what I should do about it, and then checks.

That's what people pay a trainer for. Not exercise knowledge, which has been free for fifteen years — the removal of a decision under uncertainty.

The answer I came in expecting was that none of them had built it. The answer I left with is more interesting: most of it is built, and almost none of it is routed. By week six I had found, in one of these apps, a working re-measurement that attributes a change in my body to the work I did. I found it by going and looking. Nothing in the product ever pointed at it.

Method. Fifteen screen recordings of my own use, 15 September – 27 October 2026 — cold installs, both mobility assessments, first and second sessions, the AI routine builder, sport-specific protocols, widgets, two deliberate lapses, a three-week program completed start to finish, and a self-initiated re-test. I've been a GOWOD member since 2019, which mattered: the reactivation case produced one of the sharpest findings. The tape corrected this document ten times, and nine of those corrections went in the apps' favor — including the two that landed last and changed the headline. One tester, six weeks: sharp for finding mechanisms, blunt for sizing them. Market figures in section 06 are Sensor Tower estimates, not company actuals.

02The goal

Every goal in this category is made of time

Here is every goal object I could commit to across three apps.

AppWhat I can commit toWhat it measuresWhat a missed day does to it
BendA streak goal — 3, 7, 14 or 30 daysDays completedBreaks. Intact or broken is the whole vocabulary
pliabilityA named-day sequence — three weeks, Monday to SundayRoutines, minutes, % of daysNothing. The sequence waits
GOWOD"Reach this 4 hours goal before 30 days"Hours accumulatedNot even expressible. The number only goes up

pliability and GOWOD both compute a mobility score. Neither lets me set a goal on it. Not one screen in the study offered a target state for my body — only targets for my attendance.

The purest artifact of it is Bend's progress meter, which is named after the user's body.

A progress meter is only honest if the member can say out loud what 100% means.

By that test, "5% complete" of a defined three-week program is honest — twenty-one known days, a start and an end. "Last 30 days: 3%" is a ratio with no target, which is a scold. And 1% of a Bendometer is 1% of nothing anyone can name.

Which is why nobody can price a missed day

I broke the habit deliberately, twice. The complete re-entry response in the category: pliability keeps the missed routines available and otherwise does nothing; Bend lets me spend a Streak Restore, a currency earned every seven consecutive posted days. Two mechanics, both bookkeeping. Neither tells me what the lapse cost, because neither goal object can carry a cost.

pliability's case is the subtle one. Its routines are day-named — the program overview lists all seven weekdays — but the names aren't binding. Week 3 showed "Monday done, Tuesday cued" on a Sunday, and showed exactly that again two days later. The sequence advances with me, not with the calendar.

A plan you cannot fall behind on is a plan you cannot be ahead of.

That's a real trade, and forgiveness matters in a recovery product where a rigid calendar turns a rest day into a failure. But forgiveness implemented as silence gives up the only thing a schedule is for.

A target made of time can only express an absence. A target made of a measurement expresses a consequence. Against Back/Core 53 → 65 by October 20, exactly one of two sentences is always available: the date holds and the target moves, or the target holds and the date moves. Both are things a coach says out loud. Neither needs a new feature — only a goal with a denominator.

And pliability is already holding every number it would need. One screen carries 71% complete, one week left, six routines left, Back/Core at 53, and a next test two weeks away.

The sentence it could already write

You've done 15 of 21. Six routines left and one week to do them in — and your re-test is two weeks out either way. Finish the six and you'll test having completed the program.

So the missing join isn't lapse → nudge; Bend has a nudge and it defends a counter. It's lapse → goal, and it can only be built on a goal worth lapsing against.

Marked acts (●) are the ones at least one product genuinely delivers.

Four of ten — and which four is the finding. Act 8 only joined the list in the last week of the study, only for pliability, and only for a member who goes and finds it.

Every act these products put in front of me can be performed without remembering anything.

An objective exam, an explanation and a session are all pre-producible. The assessment runs the same logic on every body; the rationale is generated on demand; the session is a video. None require the product to hold anything from one day to the next.

Every missing act — intake that informs a later decision, a goal that can be met, a plan with a stopping condition, a debrief that references last time, a graduation — requires memory. The product has to carry something across a gap and act on it later. Which is why act 8 is the one worth watching: pliability does carry the baseline across the gap and act on it. It just never tells anyone.

The daily loop decides whether I open the app tomorrow, and only the session in the middle of it is real. Look at who performs assignment there: in Bend I type what I want, in GOWOD I search the workout I already did, in pliability I sort Tennis → Hips and reason my own way to a session.

That last move is the diagnosis, and I'm the one doing it.

I know tennis loads my hips, so I pick hips. The app supplied a filing system for a conclusion I reached myself. That locates the real damage the catalog does: it isn't that the library is hard to navigate — it's that the library is where the member does the product's job.

04The three

One act each, done very well

The session is the one act all three deliver, and the only part of these products that's genuinely differentiated. Each is also strongest at a different stage — and between them they hold a complete product that none of them is.

Bend — a complete loop, closed around the wrong variable

Bend is the biggest app in the category by an order of magnitude and the easiest to underestimate. Its loop isn't shallow; it's finished — every stage built, wired and shipping — and every stage is about showing up.

Onboarding is a conversion funnel and very good at being one. Mission, then the thesis stated outright on screen three — "Consistency is key" — then the single best question in the study: "When is a good time for your daily stretch?" After waking, after coffee, after exercising, before bed. That's an implementation intention, neither competitor asks anything like it, and it's the one move I'd steal outright. Then a paywall at 2:20, purchase at 2:44, account creation at 3:00. Bend monetizes before the member has done one stretch.

That's not a shallow loop. It's a complete one pointed at the wrong thing, which is exactly why Bend's engagement is rising and its revenue per user is a fifteenth of its competitors'. The member gets precisely what the loop verifies: evidence they showed up.

The exception is Bend AI. I typed a paragraph about tight hips, thoracic tightness and wanting a better tennis serve; ninety seconds later it produced a named routine with a rationale under every exercise tied to what I asked for. Best single act of personalization in the category.

I pushed it harder a week later: same prompt style, difficulty set to Advanced, after a heavy lifting session. It returned arm circles and leg swings with an excellent rationale attached. Bend AI reasons about a selection it cannot improve. Setting the profile to Expert later produced near-identical content in a different wrapper. Difficulty is a label on one library.

Which is the line between the second and third tiers of this business. Explaining a decision is cheap now. Having a decision worth explaining is the part that isn't.

pliability — the best instrument, two taps from nowhere

pliability has built the best diagnosis in this category and one of the best prescriptions I've seen in any coaching product. I found both by accident, in an app I'd already been using for two days.

The assessment is camera-based: real-time skeletal tracking, live cues, three minutes, fully automatic. What comes out isn't a range-of-motion number — it's named movement compensations, a scored key area, and recommended zones.

pliability's in-session narration is also the best thing in the category and sits outside every framework here. A timer tells me how long; a cue tells me what to do with the time. In a two-minute passive hold there are a hundred seconds of unfilled attention — exactly when form degrades and I bail early. pliability fills it. Bend's equivalent is a tell: tapping the live demo pauses the timer, so instruction costs you progress.

GOWOD — the right questions, the worst memory

I opened GOWOD on 15 September as a member of seven years. This is what it showed me.

GOWOD otherwise has the most complete measurement apparatus in the set — six zones scored per side, so it catches left-right imbalance, plus a score history chart. It also has the crudest instrument: ten tests, twelve minutes, and a slider I drag against reference photos to grade myself.

The better instrument has the worse memory.

pliability measures me automatically and forgets. GOWOD makes me grade myself and keeps a chart. Between them they hold every part needed to close the loop.

GOWOD also asks what a clinician asks. Before a protocol it wants to know how much time I have and what equipment is with me, and builds the session around the answers. On the CrossFit flow I could search my workout — typing three letters surfaced both movements and named benchmarks, and the app already knew THE CHIEF means power cleans, push-ups and air squats. That's the endemic-community advantage made into a feature, and the closest thing in the category to a clinician asking what you actually did. Then the equipment step undercuts it: it's single-select. A real recovery session uses a roller, a ball and a band at once, and the choice isn't cosmetic — picking the percussion gun changed the session from passive stretches to massage work.

The best intake in the set, and the input is a radio button.

05Routing

Everything is built. Almost nothing routes.

This is the section that changed in the last week of the study, and it changed in the apps' favor.

Across all three the recurring failure isn't a missing feature. It's a screen with nothing routing to it, or prime real estate spent on the wrong number.

Failure modeWhat it meansOn tape
No surfaceThe capability doesn't existBend has no diagnosis of any kind
Orphaned surfaceThe screen exists and nothing routes to itpliability's assessment, program builder and re-test
Misallocated surfacePrime real estate rendering the wrong variableThe Bendometer — a body's name on an attendance meter
Competing surfacesSeveral entry points at one stage, cancelling each otherpliability: Daily Sessions and Paths and Build Your Program and Find a Session and Start a Goal and Challenges

The clearest instance is a single minute of tape. These two screens are one tab apart.

The rule I'd hold a team to

One home surface per stage, and the home screen shows at most one element from each

Anything more is a catalog. And a catalog teaches the member which mode they're in: browsing is the mode where you program yourself, which is the exact job you paid to outsource.

Three things above the fold: the next action (today's prescribed routine, named, with its duration) · where I am (Week 1, day 3 of 7) · where I'm going (Back/Core 53, retest 20 October — the target and the date the claim gets settled).

I finished the program, and graduation counted attendance

On 10 October I completed the twenty-first routine and recorded what happened — the payoff event, the highest-attention screen in the product. It's a good ceremony: a tap-to-start card, "Way to go Michael, you finished the program!", then six shareable full-screen cards reporting 5h 1m · 21 routines · 53 poses · most-frequent pose · 17 of 30 days · a Vidal Sassoon quote.

The next step offered is Create New Program: assignment without diagnosis, the loop restarting at stage two. I declined, tapped through, and landed on a Find a Path catalog of forty-odd templates — the member finishing the prescription and falling back into the catalog.

Then I ran the re-test myself, and found the loop closed

No prompt ever arrived. Five days later I went and found the test, ran it, and discovered that most of what I'd spent this teardown calling missing is built.

This is the join I spent six weeks saying the category hadn't built. Measured, re-measured, delta rendered in plain language, a compensation pattern retired, next plan offered from the new reading. It's the only causal claim about a body anywhere in this study, and pliability makes it well.

The most valuable screen in the category sits behind a door nobody knocks on.

Everything wrong with it is routing. Nothing prompted the test; the completion ceremony didn't mention it; the home screen doesn't carry it. I reached it by going to my own profile and tapping New Test — the same orphaned-surface pattern as the assessment and the program builder, except here it's the payoff rather than the setup.

Two things on that screen are the last real gaps.

  • "Most improved: Back/Core +12" and "Needs attention: Back/Core +12." Same area, same number, both slots. My biggest win is still my stated problem. The surface for Back/Core graduated — your new critical area is Hips is drawn, labelled, and filled with the old answer. Nothing closes, so no new goal opens.
  • "We currently don't have any recommended videos that match your needs." An empty state served directly beneath a fresh score, two named patterns and a scored key area — the diagnosis→assignment join firing into nothing at the moment the product knows the most it has ever known about this body.

GOWOD holds the other half of the correction: in October it pushed me to re-test. The only notification I received in six weeks aimed at an instrument rather than a streak or a session. The reminder exists; it just doesn't fire for the member who's been gone long enough to need it.

One detail I didn't plan. Earlier in this document I invented a target sentence to illustrate what a real goal would look like: Back/Core 53 → 65. I picked 65 as plausible. The re-test came back at 65. The target wasn't just settable — it was accurate. No app offered it, I hit it anyway, and I had to go look up whether I had.

The paradox, and what resolves it

Bend has the shallowest loop and the best engagement trend — daily actives up 44% on downloads up only 4.2%. Taken naively that's evidence against everything above. And Bend monetizes at $4.54 per monthly active user against GOWOD's $85.72 and pliability's $64.33.

Participation mechanics move frequency. Verification mechanics move price.

Those two facts are the same fact. Bend optimizes how many people open the app; GOWOD optimizes what an opening is worth. The loop argument is a pricing-power argument, not a DAU argument.

Frequency and intensity

In games I described engagement with two variables. Frequency is the regularity of the habit. Intensity is what that time is made of. All three compete on frequency and Bend is winning outright. Nobody competes on intensity — because intensity is being measured wrong.

A mobility session is productive; that's not in dispute. But in a coaching product, intensity isn't the quality of the session. It's how many acts of the service that time contains. By that measure a daily Bend session is one act out of ten, performed very well.

Frequency is bought with participation mechanics, and it's cheap — streaks, reminders, widgets, a rotating daily card. Bend proves it works and proves what it's worth alone: $4.54 a year per active user. Intensity is bought with memory, and that's where the price is.

How each one finds its users

The scale gap isn't a product gap, it's an acquisition-model gap. GOWOD and pliability acquire through a community — GOWOD has been Official Mobility Partner of the CrossFit Games since 2022; pliability came out of ROMWOD and became HYROX's first Official Stretching Partner in 2025. Both have reached beyond functional fitness and neither has built a second channel doing it: pliability's outside moves are individual athletes, not organizations, and of the three non-CrossFit teams on GOWOD's homepage, only Groupama-FDJ lists it back, in its lowest tier. Bend acquires through an ad account — a two-person company producing 600+ creatives across Facebook and TikTok, aimed at users 35 and older.

Each model caps on a different axis. Community is cheap and converts into sticky, high-value members — and is capped at the size of the community. Paid creative against a general audience produces lower-value members and has no ceiling: fifteen million downloads.

The onboarding follows the channel. GOWOD asks what sport I play — an identity, a narrow funnel. Bend asks what hurts and when I'll stretch — a condition, and almost everyone over 35 has one.

On pliability's decline: downloads −59.5% and actives −44.8%, but its percentage of active days is flat and its WAU/MAU is the highest of the three. What collapsed is intake, not retention. Two explanations compound — a demand-side story and a loop story — and this data can't separate them. Sensor Tower figures are modeled estimates.

07What to build

Three things, and a list of what not to do

1 · Route what already exists

The cheapest, largest fix in the study costs nothing structural. pliability's assessment, program builder and re-test are all built and all orphaned. Put the re-test where the member already is; put the program on the home screen instead of two taps down; send the notification that names the body area instead of the streak. The category is one routing layer away from being a service, not one platform away.

2 · Make the goal a measurement, and let it graduate

Every goal object here is made of time, which is why nothing can be met and nothing can be lost. Give the target a measurement and a date and both become possible. Then close it: Back/Core went 53 to 65. Your new critical area is Hips. That sentence is a new chapter opening, the surface for it is already drawn in pliability, and it currently contains the old answer.

The map that supports it isn't a winding path — it's a body diagram with zones lit by score, one marked as the current node, a target on it, and a retest date. GOWOD's six zone bars are the closest anyone gets, and they're a bar chart: no target, no next, no "you are here."

And the number has to mean something. A score of 66 tells me nothing; neither does 307 hours. Duolingo solved this with CEFR — its level screen says what level 24 lets you do. Mobility has no consumer-facing ladder, and that's the opportunity rather than the obstacle: the raw material exists in clinical and strength work (the sitting-rising test, the overhead squat assessment, the deep squat hold), and none of it has been assembled into one ladder a normal person can see themselves on. Every rung would be camera-verifiable in under a minute, and unlike language it has to run in both directions and be age-aware.

Whoever writes the ladder owns the vocabulary of the category.

3 · Build smaller units, not more content

This is where I'd put the effort, and it's the conclusion six weeks actually produced. The library, the camera, per-side measurement, AI rationale and a working re-test all already ship. The missing layer is small, frequent units of engagement that make a member feel attended to, each producing or spending a piece of state.

UnitDurationProduces or spends
Check-in scoped to the diagnosed area5sProduces a daily series on the thing the program targets
Single-movement spot-check, camera15sProduces one tracked signal, far more often than a full battery
Prescribed session10–20 minSpends the prescription
One-question debrief — "did that help?"5sProduces tomorrow's adjustment
Weekly adjustment that cites last week30s readSpends the week's data
Full re-test3 minProduces the capability claim

The test for whether a unit earns its place: does it produce data or spend it? A unit that does neither is content.

The spot-check is the one I'd build first, because it breaks the cadence deadlock. You can't re-run a composite battery weekly without wrecking test–retest reliability — but you can measure one movement every few days, because you're tracking a signal rather than re-scoring a composite. pliability already ships the skeletal tracking and spends it on a three-minute battery once a month. It's the highest-leverage unused capability in the category, by a distance.

And the daily unit needs a state input, not an attribute. These apps personalize on sport, body area and experience level — attributes that change once a year. State changes daily: what I trained yesterday, how hard, what's sore. All three already connect to Apple Health, Strava, WHOOP or Garmin and use those connections to log a session rather than choose one. Add ten seconds of self-report and you get the sentence nobody in this category can currently write:

Your hamstrings are sore, but your test says the restriction is in your hips. So today is hip work, not hamstring stretching.

Diagnosis, prescription and education in one notification — possible only because the product holds a scan from four weeks ago and a tap from this morning at once.

What not to build

  • A streak that punishes rest. Bend's Streak Restore is locked until a two-day streak — unavailable at exactly the moment most members break. Forgiveness you have to earn first isn't forgiveness.
  • A body metric that measures attendance. If the meter carries the member's body in its name, it has to be computed from the member's body.
  • A graduation ceremony that counts attendance. The most expensive screen a coaching product owns, and the one place a product holding a baseline has no excuse not to re-measure.
  • A goal set to the member's own average, or rewards for volume in a product whose thesis is restraint.
  • A diagnosis with no expiry. A stale assessment is worse than none — it prescribes confidently against a body that has changed.
  • Sharing consistency when you could share improvement. pliability's share card offers a motivational quote, in an app holding a score, named issues and a critical zone.

The gap is the product

Physical therapy in the US without insurance runs roughly $80–$150 a session, and a course of care is six to eight weeks at two to three visits a week. One session costs about what a year of pliability costs.

Software can't take palpation, manual therapy, or the clinical judgment that separates a mobility restriction from a red flag needing referral. It can take almost everything else at close to zero marginal cost: the intake that informs later decisions, the goal in functional terms, the plan with stopping criteria, the daily check-in, the spot-check, the debrief that references last session, the plan revision, the graduation. Not one needs a person in the room. Every one needs the product to remember.

And the people in these apps are overwhelmingly people who will never book a clinician — for cost, for time, or because nothing hurts badly enough yet. That's not a market being served worse than professional care. It's a market being served instead of nothing.

Which reframes the whole teardown. The finding isn't that pliability, GOWOD and Bend are bad products — they're all better than I expected, and nine of my ten corrections ran in their favor. The finding is that the category has built the content layer and most of the instruments to a high standard, has barely started on the layer that routes a member through them, and that layer is where both the price and the reach are.
