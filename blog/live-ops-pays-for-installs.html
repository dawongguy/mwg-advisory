MWG Advisory · Thesis

Live ops is how you pay for installs

The content treadmill is still running, just at higher speeds and steeper inclines.

Michael Wong October 2026 Lens: live service games & apps
01The thesis

Live ops is a UA function, not only a retention one

Live ops and economic depth are what let a game keep paying for installs when the cost of an install outruns the value of a payer.

Much of the conversation about live ops treats it as a retention function. It’s also a UA function, in that it greatly influences how much a game can bid, how long it can wait for payback, and whether it can scale spend at all.

Three mechanisms carry that argument, and all of them depend on an economy deep enough to give value away without breaking:

  1. Returners. Reactivated players carry outsized ROAS and lower blended CAC.
  2. Early-funnel monetization. Pulling LTV forward shortens payback as CPIs rise.
  3. D2C margin. Mature, habituated cohorts generate margin that subsidizes the CAC of newer cohorts.

The common assumption that audiences can refresh on their own through new product launches isn’t true anymore. The data says they don’t. Growth relies more than ever on established players in a game.

02Market context

The market is growing on depth, not reach

Newzoo forecasts $213.9B in 2026, up 6.1%. But the growth isn’t coming from new audiences. Acquisition is getting more expensive while each payer is worth about the same.

Read together, the numbers describe a squeeze. Acquisition got more expensive and less available while the average payer barely moved. Spender conversion is broadly flat, so a new install isn’t more likely to pay than it was a year ago.

The revenue that is growing comes from inside existing games. Mobile revenue is still forecast to grow 6.8% in 2026 despite the drop in downloads, and Newzoo attributes it to established games driving deeper spending from existing players.

03Returners

Returners are the ROAS that lets UA scale

The uncomfortable truth in this environment is that UA campaigns can’t fully scale without returners. They make up a minority of the installs acquired, but they contribute outsized ROAS, and that is what makes the blended economics work. Reactivation isn’t a side program next to UA. It’s part of the reason developers of older games can spend at all.

The obvious objection is incrementality: some of those players would have come back anyway through organic channels (community and Discord, push, email, or a content drop). That’s true, and it’s worth measuring. But it’s also true for every competitor bidding in the same auctions. We all regularly see ads for games and apps we already have installed, some of which we haven’t even lapsed from. Retargeting existing and former users is now a standard part of how established titles bid, which makes it part of the market price of an install.

A lapsed payer is not a lost customer. They’re a cheaper, higher-value acquisition target than a new install.

That holds as long as there is something worth coming back to, and that condition is a live ops condition. Returners respond to new events, seasons and content they missed, and to offers that help them catch up.

04Early funnel

Early-funnel monetization needs economic depth

Rising CPIs push LTV forward. When an install costs 30% more and a payer is worth the same, a game either monetizes earlier or waits longer for payback, and most UA budgets can’t wait. That’s why so many developers have moved to earlier spend depth, hybrid monetization with ads, or both.

Early monetization only works if the value proposition is real. The player has to understand what they need and why the offer is a good deal. Habby’s games do this well: the offers are dense, but the UX makes the need and the value clear at the moment of purchase.

Long-running market incumbents hold another advantage: years of content runway and economies they can afford to discount. They can repackage existing content as extra-value offers, which helps raise early LTV. Cheaper, faster early progression also usually helps early retention.

The general point is that economic depth is the safeguard. Games with deep enough economies have survived rising UA costs because they have room to give value away early without breaking progression. Games without that depth have nothing to discount. Depth isn’t the same as high cognitive load or complexity. It’s about having more things that players need (demand) and that developers can sell (supply).

05The treadmill

The cost of discounting is the content treadmill

Discounting has a cost. Players learn to wait for the deal, and repackaged value loses perceived worth as power creeps. That cost is real, but live ops is what keeps it in check.

Time pressure and fear of missing out exist because there are relevant limited-time events and seasonal content that players don’t want to fall behind on. A discount on the path to what is currently relevant in the game is worth more today than next month. Live ops gives every offer a reason to be bought now.

So the real cost of discounting is the content treadmill. The team has to extend the economy faster than players consume it. When content production slows, the discounts lose their urgency, players hit the ceiling, and banked currency has nowhere to go. The constraint isn’t the discount itself but the team’s ability to keep making new places for value to go.

06D2C

D2C margin and the cohort subsidy

Webstores are usually discussed as merchandising: better flows, better visibility, more SKUs. The harder question is how much incentive a game can offer to pull a player out of the native mobile purchase flow. That incentive is paid for out of the margin difference between app store fees and web payment costs, and it only works if the economy has room to give bonus value without inflating.

The margin D2C recovers cushions CAC increases, but it does so indirectly. D2C converts mostly engaged, habituated payers, the players a game already acquired years ago. It lengthens the payback window on the P&L as a whole rather than fixing early-funnel conversion.

None of this is new, but it matters more now. In practice, older habituated cohorts end up subsidizing part of the CAC for newer cohorts. That’s a healthy model as long as the older cohorts stay engaged, which brings it back to live ops. It’s also consistent with Newzoo’s finding that mobile revenue is decoupling from downloads.

07Implications

Why this favors incumbents

The three mechanisms compound for games that already have depth. A long-running title has returners to reactivate, a back catalog of content to discount, and habituated cohorts whose D2C margin funds new acquisition. Each one lets it bid more for an install than a new entrant can justify. That’s a supply-side explanation for why the top charts look the same year after year.

A new entrant starts with none of the three. It has no lapsed players, no content runway to repackage, and no mature cohort generating margin. Its options are narrower:

  • Build economic depth early. Design the economy for long-term live operation from launch, not as a later retrofit.
  • Make early value legible. Habby’s approach shows that clear offer-to-value UX can pull LTV forward without a long back catalog.
  • Take every organic advantage you can. An IP, a brand, a community or an existing play pattern all count.

The open question for new product developers is how long they can keep up, how much keeping up costs, and when it makes more sense to use their pipeline to put another entrant into the market.
